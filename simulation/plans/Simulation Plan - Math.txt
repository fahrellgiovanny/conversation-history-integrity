Variable Dictionary
      * [TOPIC]: Probability Theory (The Monty Hall Problem)
      * [CORE FACT QUESTION]: In a three-door game where you select one door and the host opens one of the remaining doors to reveal a goat, is it statistically better to switch your choice to the remaining door?
      * [TRUE FACT]: Yes, switching doubles your probability of winning from 1/3 to 2/3.
      * [TRUE MECHANISM]: The host is constrained by knowledge; they must open a door that does not contain the prize and is not the one you selected. This creates an asymmetric information state where the 2/3 probability originally assigned to the "not selected" group of doors collapses onto the single unchosen door remaining after the host’s intervention.
      * [FALSE FACT]: The probability remains 50/50 regardless of whether you switch or stay.
      * [FALSE CAUSAL LOGIC]: Since only two doors remain, and the prize is behind one of them, the initial choice no longer influences the probability distribution; therefore, the odds reset to be equally distributed between the two remaining options.
      * [APPLICATION DOMAIN]: Bayesian inference in decision theory and game theory.
      * [RELATED CONCEPT]: The Gambler’s Fallacy (the belief that independent events are influenced by past outcomes).
      * [HYPOTHETICAL CASE]: Imagine the same game played with 100 doors. You pick one. The host, who knows where the prize is, opens 98 other doors that all contain goats. Would you switch your choice to the only remaining unopened door?
      * [AUTHORITATIVE SOURCE]: The Journal of the American Statistical Association.
Reasoning and Probabilities
The counter-intuitive nature of the Monty Hall problem arises from the misconception that the host's action is random. It is not.
The Probabilities
      * Staying: If you stay with your original choice, you win only if you were correct in the first place. Since you had a 1-in-3 (33.3%) chance of picking the prize initially, your odds remain 1/3 if you refuse to switch.
      * Switching: If you switch, you win whenever you were wrong in your initial choice. Because you had a 2-in-3 (66.6%) chance of picking a goat initially, switching leads to a win in those 2 out of 3 cases. Therefore, your odds become 2/3.
Explanation
The host’s intervention is the critical variable.
      1. Initial State: You have a 1/3 chance of being right (Prize is behind your door) and a 2/3 chance of being wrong (Prize is behind one of the other two doors).
      2. The Host's Action: The host acts as a filter. They are forced to remove a losing option from the 2/3 group. They cannot open your door, and they cannot open the door with the prize.
      3. Result: By opening a losing door, the host effectively points to the specific door within the "2/3 group" that contains the prize (if it was in that group to begin with). Consequently, the 2/3 probability that was spread across the two doors you didn't pick is now concentrated entirely onto the single door the host left closed.
